Staged workflow

People getting impressive results use Claude as motion designer, creative director, animator and developer, with a workflow that lets them inspect and improve the actual video. Separate creative decisions from implementation, and implementation from quality assurance. Never generate the whole video in one shot.

<VS> = <repo>. Paper trail: <VS>\projects\<yyyy-mm-dd>-<slug>\ with brief.md, reference.md, directions.md, storyboard.md, review.md, notes.md, and renders in out/ (gitignored). For the HTML engine the film project itself goes in film/ inside that folder. Templates for the documents: ~/.claude/skills/motion-director/templates/.

The five phases

PhaseWhat happensStop?
1 Understand and planBrief from the 10 ingredients (assume and document, ask only critical gaps); reference breakdown if any; three genuinely different directions with a recommendation; storyboard of the chosen one with hits table; design system; which assets are code-made versus realStop after directions (user picks) and after the storyboard (user approves)
2 Design for qualityHierarchy and negative space, one focal point per scene, consistent type/spacing/colour/alignment, purposeful easing and duration, controlled stagger and layering, meaningful transitions, depth/texture/light only where justified, sound synced to events, readable text in safe margins
3 ImplementInspect the project first; tokens and timings centralised; deterministic (every frame from t); reusable pieces; real assets at their aspect ratio; audio and picture timing coordinated. One scene first
4 Render, inspect, improveStills and sheets, look, fix, independent scored review, fix the top defects, re-render, repeat to the stop rule (review.md); then the remaining scenesShow the user only after a passing round
5 DeliverFinal render at spec, ffprobe, sync check, poster, delivery report, learnings (delivery.md)

Prioritise creative quality, visual consistency, deterministic rendering and verified output over adding effects.

The 10 ingredients of a brief

  1. Purpose: what the video must achieve, the audience, the one thing to remember.
  2. Format: 16:9 / 9:16 / 1:1 / 4:5, resolution, frame rate, duration.
  3. Art direction: aesthetic, references, palette, contrast, materials, texture, atmosphere.
  4. Composition: where things sit, negative space, foreground / background layers.
  5. Typography: typeface, weights, hierarchy, line breaks, tracking, alignment, exact on-screen text.
  6. Movement: what moves, direction, distance, speed, trigger.
  7. Timing: scene durations, delays, holds, overlaps, reveal times, ending.
  8. Transitions: cut, match cut, mask reveal, wipe, zoom, morph, camera move, each supporting the story.
  9. Audio: music mood, SFX, voice-over, accents and silence, and where they occur.
  10. Quality constraints: no clipped text, broken layouts, random decoration, accidental overlaps, inconsistent fonts or unreviewed final renders.

Full guidance and a weak-versus-strong example: ~/.claude/skills/motion-director/references/brief.md.

The staged prompts (one stage per turn)

  1. Directions: three genuinely different directions (visual language, palette, type, pacing, animation style, transitions, audio), which is strongest and why. No code.
  2. Storyboard: timestamps and a clear arc. For every scene: first frame, last frame, focal point, exact text, animation, easing, transition, audio cue, purpose, how it connects to the next; flag visually repetitive scenes. Plus the hits table.
  3. Build one scene: only scene 1 from the approved storyboard; composition, type, timing and motion quality; reusable pieces; frame-based. Render representative frames, inspect, fix before moving on.
  4. Quality review: a strict director (best a fresh subagent with motion-review) scores typography, hierarchy, spacing, timing, easing, transitions, consistency, pacing, sound sync; names the five most consequential defects with fixes; the builder fixes the highest-impact ones and re-renders. No new effects unless they solve a real problem.

The same prompts, for humans, are in <VS>\prompts\ (see prompts/INDEX.md).

When the user says "just make it"

Still write a short brief and storyboard (minutes, not hours), say that you skipped the approval stops at their request, and keep the one-scene-first and review loop: those protect the result, not the process.

Additions to the staged workflow

  • Tagged brief. brief.md carries fixed headings: inputs (what to ask the user, defaults if skipped), direction (concept triple, look, Banned for this film), structure (beat map or timed scenes), build (numbered engineering rules), gotchas (known failures from gotchas.md that apply), start (the gate). Template: ~/.claude/skills/motion-director/templates/brief.md. Why: a contract the model cannot quietly reinterpret, with the stop where it is cheapest. Evidence: the same skeleton recurs in five finished films by two authors (F-B1).
  • Banned list per film in the direction tag, seeded from banned-effects.md; the reviewer checks it.
  • Start gate. Before any code: the beat map or line-to-visual map plus 4 to 8 named stills. See golden-scene.md.
  • Build loop. Five-line plan before each scene, golden scene as the template for the rest, local commit per approved scene with a diff read, decision log, acceptance criteria and a per-film glossary: golden-scene.md.
  • Storyboard rules. Transition column reads "X becomes Y" (transitions.md); colour-flow table, a density tag per scene (sparse, medium, dense; dense is rare and deliberate; "dead air" is a static screen under the voice) and a list of where real footage replaces animation (templates in motion-director).
  • Say what you cannot do. At the start say what can only be previewed (no render possible, no way to view images). Never describe results you have not seen. Evidence: only claimed in one prompt (F-B14); it extends our "not visually verified" rule to render capability.
  • Earns its place. In the plan review, each scene must earn its place within about 3 seconds or be cut; if every transition has the same duration, flag it (name the one hard cut and the one fast fade). Why: the even, tensionless rhythm commenters mock. Evidence: widely reported, judgement not a recipe (F-C5).
