Three ways to make the 20 s showreel

The film is the hero of the studio's README. In 20 seconds it has to show that a typed brief becomes a finished, reviewed motion film, made entirely in code. Below are three directions, each with three key frames drawn as rough sketches, its beat map, and an honest read of strengths, risks and effort. A comparison and a recommendation follow.

How to read the sketches: each frame is laid out at the film's real 1920 × 1080 size with the planned fonts and pixel sizes, then scaled down to fit. Click a frame to see it larger. They are sketches, not renders: real lighting, depth of field and textures come in the build.

Direction A

One take

The typed brief is the first object in one continuous 3D world. The camera never cuts: it pulls back onto a storyboard wall, pushes into one card, cranes down onto the timeline that card becomes, and watches the timeline stand up as the contact sheet of this very film, which collapses into the logo.

Found while drawing: the full brief line at 64 px mono (> 20 s showreel, one accent, every frame from code) measures about 1,920 px wide, more than the 1,680 px between the 120 px margins. Frame 0 fits, but by 2.0 s the line must break onto two rows or drop to about 54 px. Worth fixing in the storyboard.

Beat strip · 120 BPM, 10 bars of 2 s

Blue marks are the three sketched frames. No cuts at all: five shared-element hand-offs at 2.5, 7.0, 11.0, 15.0 and 18.0.

Look

Near-black #0D0E10, matte paper-like cards with 1 px borders, a 48 px grid on wall and floor at 6 %. Inter Tight 600 display at 132 px, JetBrains Mono for the brief and timecodes. Fog and contact shadows for depth; no glow, bloom or particles.

Camera

One world, one camera, 35° FOV. One named move per shot (dolly out, truck, push in, crane, orbit), expo in/out, log-space zoom, real holds at 6.0, 8.5 to 10.5 and 19.0 to 20.0. Never drifts.

Transitions

All shared elements: line becomes card one; card border becomes the frame edge; each line of type squashes into its own timeline track; tracks hinge up into contact-sheet rows; four checks merge into the dot.

Sound

Synth bed at 120 BPM, half a beat of silence at the start, key ticks on the typed words, whooshes peaking on each hand-off, seven lock ticks on keyframes, a soft low hit on the dot. Nothing depends on it.

Strengths

  • Shows the real pipeline end to end, using the film's own material: its brief, cards, waveform, frame numbers and contact sheet.
  • Follows all five inspiration rules: one thread, no cuts, shared-element hand-offs, steady camera, beat map first.
  • Frame 0 is already a readable sentence, so the hook works muted.
  • Delivers the camera moves and 3D that were asked for.

Risks

  • Cards in 3D can read as cheap "UI on slabs". Fallback: drop the yaw, keep only the truck.
  • The text-to-track squash at 10.5 s can look mushy; build and test it alone first.
  • The contact sheet needs a two-pass render of the film that contains it.
  • Many ideas for 20 s; the orbit in Review is the first thing to cut.

Effort

High. HTML engine plus three.js, about five build rounds with a review after each. Costly parts: the 10.5 s transition and the two-pass contact sheet. No external assets.

Direction B

Set in type

The whole workflow is typeset on one Swiss grid, and each word turns into the thing it names: "frame" becomes the frame, "Storyboard" cuts itself into panels, and "the code" flips over to read "rendered frames".

Found while drawing: "Every frame rendered from code." at 132 px Inter 600 measures about 1,970 px, wider than the 1,728 px grid, so it cannot sit on one baseline as written. The sketch breaks it after "frame"; the other fix is 112 px type. Also, a plane that flips 180° about the rule ends up above it, on top of "Judged on", unless that line clears first.

Beat strip · 120 BPM, 10 bars of 2 s

Shared elements inside the workflow section, two hard cuts on downbeats (12.0 and the end of the outline), one CSS 3D flip at 15.0. Last frame equals the first, so it loops.

Look

Paper #F2F1EC, ink #0D0E10, drawn 12-column hairlines that never move. Inter variable at 220, 132 and 64 px, JetBrains Mono only for skill names. Accent used once per beat and only on large shapes, since blue on paper is about 2.9:1.

Camera

No virtual camera. Scale changes stand in for it: one push (a panel scaling 1.0 to 3.2×), lines trucking along their baselines. The grid is the tripod that makes every move measurable.

Transitions

Word becomes the next scene: the box of "frame" floods to the frame, the picked weight slides into the index, panel three pushes in, the skill list squashes into the hinge rule. Two hard cuts on the beat. No crossfades.

Sound

Dry 120 BPM pulse, paper slides on each truck, five rising ticks for the skills, one silent beat before the flip and a wooden thock when it lands, a single held chord under the end card.

Strengths

  • The most phone-readable: one big line per beat, nothing small carries meaning.
  • Lowest risk and effort; DOM and CSS only, no three.js.
  • All copy is real copy from the repo, and it loops cleanly.

Risks

  • The least spatial of the three: one small flip is the only 3D, while the brief asked for camera moves and 3D.
  • Tells the workflow in words more than it shows the work.
  • A light page can look harsh in GitHub dark mode (a dark variant is a one-token swap).
  • Type-only reels are a known genre; it lives or dies on alignment craft.

Effort

Low. About 1.5 days to build plus half a day of stills, phone sheet and sync checks. HTML engine, DOM and CSS transforms driven by one render(frame) function.

Direction C

The stack

The whole film is one machined object: a stack of picture frames held in aluminium. A single accent-blue tab, the playhead, moves through the stack, and each stage of the studio is the same object lit and framed a different way. "Every frame is a function of time" becomes something you could hold.

The hero plate in C2 shows a neutral placeholder; in the film it carries a real rendered still. These sketches cannot show what decides C: whether three.js aluminium reads as metal or as grey plastic.

Beat strip · 96 BPM, 8 bars of 2.5 s

Eight shots, about 3.5 cuts per 10 s: deliberately slow. One plain cut at 12.5; the rest are hidden in the object or carried by it.

Look

One object on a seamless dark cyc. Bead-blasted aluminium (roughness about 0.45), a satin anodised blue tab, unlit picture inserts so real frames keep their colours. One soft key top left, one hard rim behind right, weak fill; lights never move.

Camera

A motion-control feel: 35 mm for wides, 85 to 100 mm for macros, one named move per shot then a hold, critically damped, never overshoots. Low hero angle of about 8°; overhead only in the sound shot.

Transitions

Macro pulls back into the reveal; a cut hidden inside a passing frame edge; the same plate shared across shots 4 and 5; one plain cut into the overhead; the closed block becomes a curtain for the end card.

Sound

Warm 96 BPM pulse, felted piano or rounded pluck, sub from bar 2. Small mechanical SFX: aluminium ticks, felt slides, one wooden clack when the stack closes, one beat of silence before it.

Strengths

  • The most premium and original: one committed object, product-film lighting, real holds.
  • A clean metaphor developers get at once: a video is an ordered stack of frames, the playhead picks one.
  • Very little text, so nothing small to read on a phone.

Risks

  • Highest technical risk: three.js metal, soft shadows, depth of field and bevel highlights all have to be right at once.
  • The first 2.5 s are a mood, not information.
  • Shows a metaphor of the pipeline more than the pipeline itself.
  • Shot 1 is a hard gate; failing it means Blender or a flatter fallback.

Effort

High. About a day to build and light the object and pass the shot 1 gate, then one to two days for the rest. Remotion with @remotion/three (a new dependency to vet) or the HTML engine with plain three.js.

Recommendation

Take A, "One take", as the spine. It is the only direction that shows the real pipeline rather than describing it, and it follows the inspiration's strongest rules directly: one thread object, no cuts, shared-element hand-offs, a steady named camera, and a beat map before pixels. It also gives you the camera moves and 3D you asked for.

Two things from the others make it better:

  • From B, words that do what they say, in the Design scene. Each line of the statement gets one typographic action tied to its meaning: Motion should lands on a spring, explain, draws its accent rule as an underline that leads the eye to the next line, and not decorate. simply cuts in on the beat with no flourish. Then, at 10.5 s, each line becomes the timeline track it feeds, which is B's "a word becomes the thing" idea carried into A's world.
  • From C, its lighting discipline as the quality bar. One key light from the top left that never moves, matte surfaces, every shot ending on a still beat. If a frame of A would not survive C's still review, it is not done.

Why not the others as the spine: C is the most premium, but it is the riskiest in three.js and stakes the whole film on materials looking real. B is the safest and the most phone-readable, but it is the least spatial, and you explicitly asked for camera moves and 3D.

Refinements to carry into the storyboard: fix the brief line so it fits the safe area (two rows, or about 54 px mono), keep the Review orbit as the first thing to cut if scene 6 feels crowded, and settle which brief wording is canonical, since the draft storyboard and A disagree slightly.

What happens next

  1. You pick A, B or C, or a mix, and say what to change.
  2. The storyboard is updated to the chosen direction, with the beat map and hits table.
  3. Scene 1 only is built as an HTML demo and reviewed by an independent reviewer on rendered stills and a phone-size sheet.
  4. Then the rest, scene by scene, each round reviewed the same way.
  5. No MP4 until you approve the HTML demo.
